I've spent the last two years cycling through budget canister filters on my own tanks — a 120-litre planted community tank and a 200-litre African cichlid setup. I wanted to find out whether you can genuinely get reliable canister filtration for under $100, or whether you're just buying yourself a headache.

The short answer: yes, there are legitimately good options in this price range. But there are also some traps. Here's what I found.

## What to Look For in a Budget Canister Filter

Not all cheap canister filters are created equal. After testing numerous budget options, here's what I've learned actually matters when you're shopping under $100.

### Flow Rate vs. Tank Volume

The general rule is 4–6 times your tank volume per hour. For a 150-litre tank, you want 600–900 L/h of rated flow. But remember — rated flow is always measured with an empty canister. Once you load it with media, expect to lose 20–30% of that figure. Always buy slightly more flow than you think you need.

### Media Tray Count and Capacity

Three trays is the sweet spot. It lets you run a proper three-stage setup: coarse mechanical filtration on the bottom tray (where water enters), biological media in the middle, and fine mechanical or chemical media on top. Two-tray filters force you to compromise, and that compromise usually means less biological capacity.

### Impeller Material

This is the single biggest differentiator in longevity at this price point. Ceramic impeller shafts last years. Plastic shafts wear down, develop wobble, and start rattling within 12–18 months. Always check — the product listing often won't mention it, but user reviews will.

### Self-Priming

A self-priming button is a genuine quality-of-life feature. Without it, you're siphon-starting the filter every time you do maintenance — which means either sucking on a hose full of tank water or fiddling with a priming method. Filters that self-prime with a push button save you hassle every single water change.

### Seal Quality

The O-ring seal between the canister body and the lid is the most common failure point on budget filters. A good O-ring sits in a well-machined groove and compresses evenly when the lid clips engage. A bad one requires silicone grease to seal and may still weep over time. This is hard to assess before purchase, but user reviews mentioning leaks are a red flag.

### Noise Level

Budget canister filters range from near-silent to genuinely annoying. Anything under 40 dB is generally fine for a living space. Above that, you'll hear it — especially at night. Rattling impellers, vibrating lids, and humming motors are all common complaints at the low end of the market.

### Spare Parts Availability in Australia

This is where Australian buyers need to pay extra attention. A filter with no local spare parts means that when the impeller wears out or an O-ring perishes (and they will, eventually), you're either ordering from overseas with 3–6 week shipping or binning the whole unit. Aqua One has the best parts network in Australia. SunSun parts are available on Amazon AU and eBay. Anything more obscure may be a gamble.
