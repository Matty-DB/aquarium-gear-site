## Best Light for Low Tech Planted Tank: Tested and Ranked

Finding the best light for a low tech planted tank shouldn't be complicated — but somehow, it is. You don't need the blinding output of a high-tech CO2 setup, but you also can't just slap any old LED strip over your tank and expect your crypts and anubias to thrive.

I've been keeping low tech planted tanks for over eight years now, and I've gone through more lights than I'd like to admit. Some were brilliant. Others turned my tanks into algae farms within a fortnight. The sweet spot for low tech is surprisingly narrow: enough PAR to grow easy-to-moderate plants, but not so much that you're battling green water and staghorn without CO2 injection to back it up.

After running six popular lights across my 60-litre, 120-litre, and 240-litre setups over the past year, here are my honest recommendations.

## 1. Fluval Plant 3.0 LED — Best Overall Light for Low Tech Planted Tanks

The Fluval Plant 3.0 has been sitting on top of my 120-litre community tank for six months now, and it's genuinely hard to fault. This is the light I recommend to anyone who wants to set it up once and not think about it again.

The standout feature is the Bluetooth app control. You get six independently adjustable colour channels — including dedicated red, blue, and a proper full-spectrum white — which means you can dial the output down to exactly what your low tech tank needs. I run mine at about 60% intensity on a 7-hour photoperiod, and my java fern, anubias nana, and cryptocoryne wendtii are all growing steadily without a whisper of algae.

The aluminium housing feels properly solid — not the flimsy plastic you get on budget lights — and the extendable mounting brackets fit tanks from 61cm to 91cm on the 24-inch model. Heat dissipation is excellent; even after 8 hours running, the housing is only warm to the touch, not hot.

**Pros:**
- Six independent colour channels let you fine-tune spectrum for low tech growth (I keep reds at 40%, blues at 30%)
- Bluetooth app includes pre-set "Tropical" and "Planted" profiles that work as sensible starting points
- Aluminium housing with proper heat sinks — no plastic creaking or warping after months of use
- Extendable brackets use rubber-tipped feet that grip the glass rim without scratching
- IP67 water-resistant rating — I've splashed it during water changes without issue

**Cons:**
- Bluetooth range is limited to about 3 metres — the app disconnects if you walk to the other side of the room
- No Wi-Fi or smart home integration — you can't control it remotely or via Alexa/Google Home
- The 24-inch model produces more PAR than most low tech tanks need at full power; you'll be dimming it to 50–70%
- At $200+ AUD from most Australian retailers, it's a meaningful investment for a low tech setup

**Who it's best for:** Anyone willing to spend a bit more for a light they can precisely tune and forget about. If you're growing crypts, swords, java fern, or other undemanding plants, the Fluval Plant 3.0 at reduced intensity is as good as it gets.

**Rating: 4.7/5**

## 2. Chihiros C2 RGB — Best for Small Low Tech Planted Tanks

I picked up the Chihiros C2 RGB specifically for my 30-litre nano tank, and it punches well above its weight. The colour rendition on this little light is genuinely impressive — my cherry shrimp and betta colours pop in a way they didn't under my old white-only LED strip.

The magnetic mounting base is clever. It clamps to tanks with glass up to 8mm thick, and you can angle the light head about 120 degrees. I've got mine tilted slightly forward to hit the foreground plants more evenly. The My Chihiros app gives you RGB channel control and a programmable sunrise/sunset cycle, which is the same app platform their professional-grade WRGB II uses.

At around 85 µmol PAR at 30cm depth, it sits right in the sweet spot for low tech — enough for monte carlo as a slow-growing carpet (without CO2, expect 3–4 months to fill in), and not so much that you'll get diatom blooms in the first week.

**Pros:**
- RGB colour control produces noticeably better plant and fish colours than white-only LEDs
- Magnetic mount is genuinely elegant — no ugly brackets or clamps visible from the front
- 18-watt output is well-matched to tanks under 40 litres without needing heavy dimming
- The app's 24-hour cycle simulation actually works — my shrimp are visibly more active during the gradual sunrise ramp

**Cons:**
- Only suitable for tanks up to about 45cm long — the spread falls off sharply past that
- Magnetic mount doesn't work on rimless tanks thicker than 8mm (check your glass before buying)
- Stock availability in Australia is hit-and-miss — I ordered mine from an online specialist; Petbarn doesn't carry Chihiros
- The power adapter uses a barrel connector that could be more robust; it wiggles slightly in the socket

**Who it's best for:** Nano tank keepers who want proper colour spectrum control without buying a $200+ light. If your tank is under 40 litres, this is the one.

**Rating: 4.5/5**

**Pros:**

## What to Look For in a Low Tech Planted Tank Light

Before you pick a light, it's worth understanding what actually matters for low tech setups. Here's what I look for:

### PAR Output (Not Just Wattage)

Forget about watts — they measure power consumption, not useful light for plants. What matters is PAR (Photosynthetically Active Radiation), measured in µmol/m²/s. For low tech planted tanks without CO2 injection, you want **30–80 µmol at substrate level**. Go higher and you're inviting algae without the CO2 and fertilisation to back it up.

### Colour Temperature

Most planted tank lights sit between 6500K and 8000K. Lower values (6500K) appear warmer and more natural. Higher values (7000–8000K) look cooler and crisper but can feel clinical. For low tech, anything in this range works — it's more about personal preference than plant growth.

### Dimmability

This is non-negotiable for me in a low tech setup. Most lights are designed to also serve high-tech tanks, so at full power they'll overwhelm a low tech system. Being able to dim to 40–70% gives you control over algae pressure without needing a less capable light.

### Timer Functionality

Built-in timers are convenient but not essential. A $12 mechanical timer from Bunnings does the same job. What matters is consistency — your plants need a regular photoperiod of 6–8 hours for low tech.

### Build Quality and Heat Management

Cheap lights with poor heat dissipation degrade faster. Aluminium housings outperform plastic for thermal management. In my experience, budget plastic lights lose about 15–20% of their brightness within 18 months, while well-built aluminium units hold steady for years.

### Spread and Coverage

A light that's brilliant directly underneath but drops off sharply at the edges will give you uneven growth. Check reviews and PAR maps (many manufacturers now publish these) to ensure decent coverage across your entire tank footprint.

## Final Verdict

For most people setting up a low tech planted tank, the **Fluval Plant 3.0 LED (4.7/5)** is the best all-round choice. The app-controlled dimming lets you dial in exactly the right intensity, the build quality is excellent, and it's readily available from Australian retailers like Petbarn and independent aquarium shops.

On a tighter budget, the **Nicrew ClassicLED Plus (4.2/5)** is hard to beat for the price — pair it with a cheap plug timer and you're sorted for under $60 AUD.

For nano tanks under 40cm, the **Chihiros C2 RGB (4.5/5)** offers the best combination of colour control and compact design, though you may need to order it online from a specialist retailer.

If you're an aquascaper who wants the absolute best visual presentation, the **Twinstar S Series (4.4/5)** produces the most beautiful light of anything on this list — just be prepared to pay for it and source it from specialist stores.

The **ONF Flat Nano Plus (4.3/5)** is a premium nano alternative with outstanding build quality, while the **Hygger 24/7 (4.0/5)** is the ultimate set-and-forget option if simplicity is your top priority.

Whichever light you choose, remember the golden rule of low tech lighting: **less is more**. You can always increase intensity gradually, but dealing with an algae outbreak from too much light is a far bigger headache. Start at 50% brightness, run a 7-hour photoperiod, and adjust from there.
