## What Exactly Is Fluval Stratum?

Fluval Stratum is a lightweight volcanic soil substrate collected from the mineral-rich foothills of Mount Aso in Japan. It comes as irregularly shaped, dark granules roughly 2–4 mm in diameter. The granules are porous, which gives beneficial bacteria loads of surface area to colonise — a genuine advantage for biological filtration.

Unlike inert substrates like plain gravel or sand, Stratum actively interacts with your water chemistry. It buffers pH downward (typically landing between 6.0 and 6.8 depending on your tap water) and provides a mildly acidic, soft-water environment. This makes it particularly popular for Caridina shrimp species and plants that prefer slightly acidic conditions.

### Key Specifications

- **Material:** Volcanic soil (Mount Aso, Japan)
- **Available sizes:** 2 kg, 4 kg, 8 kg bags
- **Grain size:** Approximately 2–4 mm, irregular
- **pH buffering:** Lowers to approximately 6.0–6.8
- **Colour:** Dark brown to black when wet
- **Recommended depth:** 5–8 cm for planted tanks

## My Experience Setting Up Fluval Stratum

### First Impressions and the Dust Problem

Let's address the elephant in the room: the initial cloudiness. When I first added Stratum to my 60-litre tank, the water turned into what I can only describe as chocolate milk. Fluval says not to rinse it, and honestly, rinsing doesn't help much anyway — the granules are so soft that washing them just creates more dust.

My approach was to fill the tank extremely slowly using a colander placed over the substrate, letting the water trickle in over about 45 minutes. Even with this method, the water was murky for a solid 36 hours before my hang-on-back filter (running filter floss as a polishing pad) cleared it up. In my shrimp tank, which runs a sponge filter only, it took closer to three days.

This is genuinely my biggest gripe with Stratum. If you're planning a scape and want to see results immediately, budget for at least two to three days of cloudy water.

### Planting Experience

Once settled, Stratum is genuinely pleasant to plant in. The granules are light enough that you can push stem plants in with tweezers without much resistance, but they hold roots reasonably well once established. My Rotala rotundifolia and Ludwigia repens anchored within about a week.

Smaller foreground plants like Monte Carlo were a different story. Because the granules are so lightweight, the tiny plantlets kept floating up for the first fortnight. I ended up using small stainless steel mesh to pin them down until the roots took hold. Not a dealbreaker, but worth knowing if you're planning a carpet.

Cryptocoryne wendtii, on the other hand, absolutely thrived. The roots spread through the Stratum easily, and I noticed noticeably faster growth compared to the Flourite Black in my other tank during the first three months.

## Performance Over Time

### Nutrient Content and Longevity

Here's where I need to set realistic expectations. Fluval markets Stratum as containing "mineral-rich" volcanic nutrients, and that's technically true — but the nutrient content is modest compared to dedicated aqua soils like ADA Amazonia.

In my planted nano, I started seeing slower growth in demanding plants around the five-month mark. My Alternanthera reineckii, which had been putting out vibrant red leaves, started looking washed out. Adding root tabs (I use Osmocote+ capsules, about $12 AUD for a year's supply) brought the colour back within two weeks.

For a low-tech setup with undemanding plants — java fern, anubias, cryptocorynes, and mosses — the base nutrients in Stratum are probably sufficient for 8–12 months. For anything medium-tech or above, plan on supplementing with root tabs from month four or five onwards.

### pH Buffering Capacity

The pH buffering is Stratum's genuine standout feature for shrimp keepers. My tap water here in Melbourne sits around 7.2–7.4, and Stratum pulled it down to a stable 6.4 in both tanks within the first week.

After eight months in the planted nano, the buffering has weakened slightly — I'm now reading 6.6–6.8. Based on reports from other hobbyists and my own trajectory, I'd estimate the buffering capacity lasts 12–18 months before it becomes essentially inert. This is fairly standard for volcanic soil substrates, though ADA Amazonia tends to hold its buffering a touch longer in my experience.

### Granule Integrity

The granules do break down over time. After eight months, I'd estimate 10–15% of the top layer has crumbled into finer sediment. This isn't catastrophic, but it does mean the substrate compacts slightly. During water changes, I'm more careful with the siphon now — aggressive gravel vacuuming will suck up the broken-down particles and make a mess.

I wouldn't plan on reusing Stratum after a full tear-down. After 18–24 months, most of the granules will have degraded enough that the substrate is essentially spent. This is a running cost to factor in.

## Fluval Stratum for Shrimp

This is where Stratum really earns its reputation. My Crystal Red Shrimp colony has been thriving on it. The combination of stable acidic pH, the soft granules that don't damage delicate shrimp legs, and the porous surface that grows biofilm (which shrimplets graze on constantly) makes it close to ideal.

The dark colour also makes the shrimp pop visually — my CRS look absolutely stunning against the near-black substrate.

For Neocaridina species, Stratum works perfectly fine too, though it's arguably overkill since Neos are less fussy about pH. If you're keeping Cherry Shrimp in a community tank, you could save some money with an inert substrate and not notice much difference.

## How It Compares to the Alternatives

### ADA Amazonia Ver. 2

If Stratum is the reliable daily driver, ADA Amazonia is the performance car. Amazonia has significantly richer nutrient content — my plants grow noticeably faster in Amazonia during the first six months. The granules are also more uniform and hold their structure longer.

The tradeoff? Amazonia leaches ammonia heavily during the first 2–4 weeks of cycling. I measured 4+ ppm ammonia in the first week of my Amazonia tank. You absolutely cannot add livestock during this period. Stratum, by contrast, leaches minimal ammonia — I had shrimp in within 10 days after a fishless cycle.

Amazonia is also harder to source in Australia. Most local fish shops don't carry it — I ordered mine from Aquotix, and it took about a week to arrive. Pricing is steeper too, typically $55–70 AUD for a 9-litre bag versus $35–45 AUD for 8 kg of Stratum at Petbarn.

### Seachem Flourite Black

Flourite is the budget long-term option. It's a porous clay gravel that never breaks down — I've seen tanks running Flourite for five-plus years without replacement. It doesn't buffer pH at all (completely inert), which is either a pro or a con depending on your needs.

The downside is that Flourite has virtually no nutrient content out of the bag. It relies entirely on absorbing nutrients from the water column over time, so you'll need root tabs and liquid fertilisers from day one. The grain size is also larger and more angular — not ideal for bottom-dwelling shrimp or Corydoras.

The initial dust from Flourite is even worse than Stratum, by the way. I rinsed mine five times and still had milky water for two days.

## What to Look For When Choosing a Planted Substrate

Before committing to any substrate, consider these factors:

### Tank Inhabitants
If you're keeping Caridina shrimp, pH-buffering substrates like Stratum or Amazonia are strongly recommended. For fish-only or Neocaridina setups, inert substrates work fine.

### Plant Demands
High-demand plants (carpeting species, red plants, fast-growing stems) benefit from nutrient-rich substrates. Low-demand plants (anubias, java fern, mosses) grow fine on anything.

### Longevity Expectations
Aqua soils and volcanic substrates degrade over 1–2 years. If you don't want to replace your substrate, choose a fired clay option like Flourite or Tropica Substrate.

### Depth and Coverage
For a standard planted tank, aim for 5–8 cm depth. A 60-litre tank (roughly 60 × 30 cm footprint) needs approximately 8–10 kg of substrate. Price accordingly — one 8 kg bag of Stratum is usually just enough for a 60-litre setup.

### Budget
Substrate is a recurring cost if you use aqua soils. Factor in replacement every 18–24 months. Inert substrates cost more effort (fertiliser dosing) but less money long-term.
